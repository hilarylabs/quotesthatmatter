**I Wish Someone Told Me This in My 20s**

![Epictetus Quotes on Letting go](/img/some-thing-within-our-power.webp)

> Some things are within our power, while others are not. Within our power are opinion, motivation, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever is of our own doing; not within our power are our body, our property, reputation, office, and, in a word, whatever is not of our own doing. — <a href="https://quotesthatmatter.com/stoic/epictetus-quotes.html" target="_blank">Epictetus</a>

This is the Dichotomy of Control — the core Stoic idea that real peace comes from putting your energy only into what's actually yours: your thoughts, your effort, your actions. Everything else — how people see you, what they feel about you or other people, how things turn out — was never yours to begin with.

I learned this the hard way, and I mean the hard way.

Growing up, I was fed love stories from movies and dramas. The girl always starts out cold, the guy chases anyway, and persistence eventually wins her over. There is some truth to it. But not as much as I expected.
When I was twenty-something, hot-blooded, with unstable emotions, you get one very confident, very wrong conclusion: if I want her, I just need to try hard enough, long enough, and eventually she'll come around.
Oh boy, how wrong I was.

When I was in my 20s, there was this one girl I liked. I really liked her. She was from my circle of friends, unofficially introduced to me because her circle overlapped with mine. Smart, pretty, always smiling. I'd bet anyone who met her liked her.

So eventually I started to pursue her. And she said no. Friends only, she said. But the movies and dramas say otherwise. If I chase long enough, if I'm persistent enough, she'll eventually fall for me. And that's exactly what I did — because I thought, yeah, I can do this, I can control the outcome.

Oh boy, if only I'd read what <a href="https://quotesthatmatter.com/stoic/marcus-aurelius-quotes.html" target="_blank">Marcus Aurelius</a> wrote back then:
> If you are distressed by anything external, the pain is not due to the thing itself, but to your estimate of it — and this you have the power to revoke at any moment.

So I chased and chased. Refused to take no as final. Convinced myself she was the one, and that belief consumed me so completely it blocked out the obvious truth staring me in the face.

In the movies, the girl eventually falls for the guy because he "put in the effort" and never surrendered. In my case, however, she never did.

Looking back now, at 40, I actually cringe. If I could go back in time, I'd be genuinely embarrassed at what I did. Like, a lot.

Here's what took me two decades to understand: chasing someone who doesn't want to be caught isn't romantic, it's just a waste of time and energy dressed up as devotion. If she likes you, she likes you. If she doesn't, you could die trying and she still wouldn't. No amount of effort converts a "no" into a "yes," because her feelings were never a project I could work on — they were hers, not mine, not ever.

The idea that she'll eventually come around only exists in the movies. In real life, it's rarely the case.

That's the part I got backwards for years. I was pouring everything I had into the one thing I had zero control over, while barely paying attention to the only things I actually did control — my own actions, my own growth, my own choices about who deserved my energy in the first place.

And it's not just relationships. It's everything in life.

Epictetus said it plainly: Within our power are opinion, motivation, desire, aversion — in a word, whatever is of our own doing.

The outcome of a game. Whether you get the job after the interview. People's opinion of you. Even the loss of someone you love. All of it is beyond our control. There's no switch we can flick to overturn the result into what we wanted.

All of it works the same way — you control the effort, not the result. Chasing the result instead of owning the effort is how you end up 40 years old, looking back at your 20s, wincing.

Letting go isn't giving up. It's finally putting your energy where it was always supposed to go — into what's actually yours to control.

It's not resignation, it's redirection: the same fire I used to burn chasing something that was never mine, now goes into the things that are — how I show up, what I build, who I choose to become. That's the only chase that was ever worth running.
