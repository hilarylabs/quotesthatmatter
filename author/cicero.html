About Cicero

Who is Cicero?

Marcus Tullius Cicero (106 BC – 43 BC) was a Roman statesman, lawyer, scholar, philosopher, and orator who lived during the final years of the Roman Republic. He is considered one of Rome's greatest prose writers and public speakers, and the originator of what became known as Ciceronian rhetoric — a style of oratory that influenced Western literature for centuries.

What is Cicero best known for?

Cicero is best known for his speeches and philosophical writings. As a lawyer and senator, he prosecuted the corrupt governor Verres and exposed the Catilinarian conspiracy that threatened the Roman Republic. As a philosopher, he introduced Greek Stoic and Academic philosophy to a Roman audience through works such as De Officiis (On Duties), Tusculan Disputations, and De Finibus. His letters, collected after his death, remain one of the most vivid personal accounts of Roman political life.

Why was Cicero assassinated?

Cicero was assassinated on December 7, 43 BC, on the orders of Mark Antony. After Julius Caesar's assassination in 44 BC, Cicero delivered a series of speeches called the Philippics, harshly attacking Antony's bid for power. When Antony formed the Second Triumvirate with Octavian and Lepidus, Cicero was placed on the proscription list — effectively a death warrant. He was caught fleeing Rome and killed by soldiers near his villa in Formiae. Antony reportedly had his hands cut off and his head displayed in the Roman Forum as a warning.

What are Cicero's most famous quotes?

Some of Cicero's most enduring quotes include "Gratitude is not only the greatest of virtues, but the parent of all others," "The life of the dead is placed in the memory of the living," and "The whole future lies in uncertainty: live immediately." His words on duty, virtue, friendship, and the nature of a good life remain widely read and quoted today.

Was Cicero a Stoic?

Cicero was not a strict Stoic but was deeply influenced by Stoic philosophy. He identified more with the Academic Skeptic tradition, which acknowledged that certainty is difficult to attain. However, he admired Stoic ethics — particularly the emphasis on virtue, duty, and reason — and helped transmit Stoic ideas to the Roman world and, through his writings, to later Western thought. Many of his most famous quotes reflect Stoic values even if he did not fully adopt the school.
